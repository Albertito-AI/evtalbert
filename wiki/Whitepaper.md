# EVT-Albert: reflective practice without reducing a person to a score

## The opportunity

A difficult performance rarely depends on one observable outcome. A person may prepare carefully, encounter an obstacle, change strategy and learn something useful even when the immediate result is disappointing. EVT-Albert proposes a simple foundation for studying that process: record sessions, describe obstacles and regulation methods, retain outcomes and inspect patterns in one's own records.

The most valuable version of this project would support thoughtful reflection and better questions. It would help a user notice what happened, what they tried and what they might test next. It would not claim that a dashboard can measure a person's worth, diagnose a condition or convert a theory of volition into a validated trait score.

Prepared for Albert Tan Sin Hon with AI assistance on 2 November 2026. This is a design and research-direction paper. It is not psychological validation, clinical advice or a claim that the proposed Volitional Index predicts real-world performance.

## First principles: a record is an observation made in context

Self-reported data are meaningful because somebody interprets their experience, not because interpretation disappears. A rating of persistence may depend on task difficulty, fatigue, expectations and the person's understanding of the scale. Missing sessions may be systematically different from recorded sessions. Comparing two people without examining those conditions risks comparing unlike things.

The first principle is therefore contextual integrity. Retain the task, time, obstacle, method and supplied ratings alongside any derived indicator. The second is user agency: the person should control which records are created, retained, exported and deleted. The third is epistemic modesty: arithmetic over a questionnaire does not establish that the questionnaire measures a stable psychological construct.

The current proposed Volitional Index uses inputs concerning purposefulness, persistence, self-control and decisiveness. Those labels express a theoretical proposal. The software can compute from them while the validity, reliability and consequences of that interpretation remain open research questions.

## Fundamental logic of the current prototype

The archived API records sessions, computes simple derived metrics, writes a Markdown summary and exports CSV. The published demonstration runs three invented sessions through that API. Its output establishes that the code performs the recorded arithmetic on fictional records. The example does not show that users became more resilient, productive or psychologically healthy.

The repository still preserves its legacy version in an archive. Generated bytecode, inconsistent old package metadata and unusual Markdown separators remain documented maintenance defects. Archive-level checks inspect digest, paths, compilation and a smoke test. Those checks support a bounded software claim; an ordinary, inspectable source tree is the necessary next foundation for reliable development.

The MIT licence permits reuse under its terms. It does not make real emotional or performance records public. The public software/private-record distinction is fundamental: a reproducible synthetic fixture can support development without exposing a person's life history.

## A useful reflective loop

Consider a fictional teacher preparing a difficult lesson. The record might describe anticipatory tension, an interruption, a chosen regulation method and the eventual teaching outcome. After several sessions, the teacher could notice that a particular preparation routine coincides with fewer abandoned plans. That is a useful question for further observation, not proof of causation.

A better interface would help the user distinguish the observation from the explanation: “These sessions used this routine” is different from “This routine caused improvement.” It would allow qualitative notes and alternative explanations rather than force every experience into a single number. The next experiment could be small, voluntary and reversible.

The tool should also support stopping. More logging is not always better. A user may find tracking burdensome, intrusive or counterproductive. The design should make pausing, deleting and exporting straightforward, rather than treating constant engagement as success.

## Social aim and foreseeable misuse

The immediate aim is accessible, private self-observation for training and performance. The larger hope is to help people learn from experience across sport, teaching, advocacy, research and other demanding work, while retaining control over their information.

That hope is incompatible with compulsory worker ranking, covert monitoring or high-stakes selection based on an unvalidated index. An apparently objective score can acquire institutional authority far beyond its evidence. The project should explicitly separate personal reflection from employment, insurance, educational selection or medical decisions. New uses would require their own justification and evaluation.

Accessibility also matters. A useful system should accommodate different vocabularies, languages and ways of describing experience. It should not assume that frequent logging, a particular emotional style or a high numerical rating is the universal ideal.

## Ideal progression

| Stage | Intended improvement | Evidence needed |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Transparent software | Extract and maintain ordinary source files | Preserved archive provenance, corrected metadata and reproducible source tests |
| Dependable personal records | Clear data contract, exports and deletion | Round-trip checks, retention behaviour and understandable permissions |
| Useful reflection | Better questions and low-burden workflows | Voluntary user observations, misunderstanding and burden records |
| Evaluated measurement | Test the proposed index rather than assume it | Appropriate construct, reliability and validity studies with uncertainty |
| Responsible wider use | Carefully bounded educational or research applications | Purpose-specific consent, governance and independent evaluation |

The ideal wishlist includes user-defined reflection prompts, multidimensional trends, explicit missingness, accessible exports, local encryption options, transparent calculations and a comparison between numeric and narrative approaches. It should preserve raw observations when an index changes, with versioned derivations rather than rewriting historical meaning.

## Evaluation and defeat conditions

Software evaluation should check correctness, safe export, malformed records, reproducibility and deletion. Usability evaluation should examine whether people understand the distinction between a reported state and an inferred trait. Research evaluation should predefine outcomes and avoid treating correlations in a self-selected log as causal effects.

Record adverse experiences as well as favourable feedback. If the index gives unstable or misleading interpretations, it should be revised or removed. If a simpler journal helps users as much with less burden, that is a valuable result. If people feel pressured to perform for the metric, the design is undermining its purpose.

The best case is a tool that strengthens reflective agency: people gain a clearer account of their own practice and remain free to disagree with the software's interpretation. Helping a person ask a better question can be a more responsible contribution than issuing a confident score.
